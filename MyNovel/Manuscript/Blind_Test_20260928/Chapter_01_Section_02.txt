The air from the open window was warm and smelled like gasoline and hot pavement as Tiffany’s car rolled slowly through the pump lane. The sun hung low and heavy over the gas station, painting everything in a soft, golden light.

Maya adjusted the strap of her backpack. “You sure you’re okay?” she asked, glancing over.

“I’m fine,” Tiffany said, leaning against the hood of the car and looking toward the convenience store. She had already finished her soda. “Just wanted to say hi to the guy who worked us on Saturday.”

“Hey.” An older man with a grease-stained apron waved from behind the counter of the small store. His eyes crinkled at the corners.

“Hi, Mr. Henderson,” Tiffany called back. She moved away from the car and walked toward the door.

“You two always come here,” he said when they stepped inside. He handed Tiffany a bottle of water without asking. “Your mother always bought six bottles at once. Said she’d drink one after every meal for good luck.”

Tiffany took the water and gave him a quick hug before turning around. Maya was right behind her.

“She meant well,” Maya said quietly, though there was no one else around to hear it.

The bell above the door jingled softly. Inside, the store was quiet except for the hum of the refrigerators and the occasional click of someone opening a snack bag. The air smelled like salt and old paper.

They stood near the cooler, sharing their sodas in silence for a moment. Then Maya spoke again, voice low. “You think we’ll ever see your mom again?”

“Nope,” Tiffany answered honestly. “But I talk about her all the time. And that helps.”

Maya nodded slowly. “I know.”

Outside, the late afternoon sun had dipped lower now, casting long shadows across the parking lot. A few cars passed slowly on the main road beyond the station. Somewhere nearby, a dog barked once and then went quiet.

Tiffany leaned against the car again, pulling out her phone. She didn’t turn it on—just looked at the lock screen. On it was a photo of her and Maya from last summer, standing in front of the same gas station, both laughing into the camera.

“Hey,” Maya said from beside her. “You got a picture here of us?”

“Yeah,” Tiffany replied. “Want me to send it to you?”

“I’d like that,” Maya said, smiling. “Thank you.”

“Anytime,” Tiffany said, unlocking the phone quickly. She tapped through her gallery and selected the photo.

As she did, Mr. Henderson appeared behind them with two large bags stacked in his arms. He set one down by the car door and held the other up.

“Here you go,” he said, handing over the second bag. “And I brought you something extra.”

Inside the bag was a small, wrapped box tied with red ribbon. Tiffany opened it carefully. Inside were four paper daisies, each pressed flat between waxed cards.

“Your mom used to give these to people when they came to visit,” he said softly. “Said they carried good memories.”

Tiffany took the flowers gently. They smelled faintly of rain-dampened grass.

“I’ll keep them,” she said.

Maya reached out and touched the edge of the box. “We should make sure we come back here often,” she murmured.

“Always,” Tiffany agreed, placing the flowers in Maya’s hands.

They stepped back into the parking lot together, the late sun warming their backs as they walked toward the car. The world felt quiet around them—the kind of quiet that came after too many things had been shared and nothing needed to be said anymore.
