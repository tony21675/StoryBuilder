The purchased snacks and sodas were tucked securely under an arm—Maya taking the heavier bag of drinks while Tiffany held onto the chips and change. With the artificial brightness of the gas station fading behind them, they resumed their pace, the rhythm settling back into the comfortable cadence of two people who knew each other’s footsteps almost by heart.

"Seriously though," Tiffany mused, adjusting her grip on the small bag, "are you going to talk to Mom about applying for any scholarships? Like, anything remotely academic sounding?" She nudged Maya lightly with her hip as they walked. "You know how stressed she gets about college applications, and if we don't get some good grades this semester, I swear she'll start cornering us about early admission essays like they're mandatory taxes."

Maya chuckled, a soft, breathy sound that carried well in the relative quiet of the residential street. "I'm talking to her. We just need to keep things steady until senior year starts messing everything up. It's easier said than done when she sees your transcripts popping up." She paused, then dropped her voice conspiratorially, making sure the words felt shared between them. "Besides, I think you've got enough stuff to stress about with the State History project anyway. Focus there first, okay? Let Mom handle the scholarship jargon."

Tiffany laughed again, the sound light and bright against the backdrop of passing lawn sprinklers kicking up faint mist. "Easy for you to say. You always have that calm aura, even when people are panicking about deadlines. You’re built different, M."

"It's not an aura; it's extreme boredom," Maya admitted wryly, pushing a strand of dark brown hair behind her ear. "Having all my spare time before finals means I’ve achieved Zen-like levels of patience. Which is useful because I can listen to you complain about glitter glue brain power for hours."

They slowed slightly as the sidewalk curved gently around a corner, the houses starting to become more varied—some pristine, some showing the character wear of years. The scent of fresh-cut grass mixed with the fading sweetness of vanilla from Maya, contrasting nicely with Tiffany's signature coconut and strawberry notes.

"Speaking of things being nice," Tiffany started, turning her body slightly toward Maya as they walked past a mailbox adorned with seasonal trinkets, "do you think we ever get sick of this neighborhood? Like, if we were going to move somewhere completely different... where would it be?"

Maya considered this, her expression thoughtful. Her eyes scanned the neat row of lawns before settling on Tiffany. "Somewhere less... predictable? Maybe someplace with more weird shops and less HOA enforcement." She tapped her chin lightly. "I always pictured us in something vaguely coastal, but gritty. Like, a place near water, but not aggressively touristy. More moody."

"Moody," Tiffany echoed, mimicking the word with a dramatic nod. "So, like a perpetually overcast New England town that smells faintly of salt and existential dread?"

Maya’s smile widened, finding the exaggeration funny. "Exactly! We could run coffee shops that only serve drinks named things like 'Regret' or 'Last Chance Summer.' And the clientele would be wonderfully dramatic."

The banter flowed effortlessly, easily filling the space between them, pulling them further down the sidewalk. They drifted into an anecdote about a mutual acquaintance from school—a story involving questionable fashion choices at a recent gathering, which sparked another round of shared giggling.

As they rounded the corner onto the street that seemed to mark their general path toward home, the residential feel began to give way again. The houses stayed mostly two-story, but the streetscape started hinting at the edge of commercial life once more; utility poles were more numerous, and the pavement seemed to stretch out over a slightly wider thoroughfare than before.

They slowed their pace again, their conversation softening as if sensing the shift in environment.

It was then, as they reached what felt like the junction between quiet suburbia and the minor business park adjacent to the gas station complex, that Maya subtly tilted her head, her observant nature catching something in the periphery. Her brow furrowed just slightly, enough for Tiffany to notice.

Tiffany followed the line of Maya's gaze, peering past the parked cars and the edge of the curb where the properties began to thin out. At first, she didn't see it either—just more mundane traffic noise, the distant whine of a lawnmower. But Maya shifted her weight, and Tiffany caught the same subtle tension in her posture.

Then, through the gap between a large oak tree and a parked sedan, it appeared: a van. It was an unremarkable, boxy vehicle, the kind you forget until it’s right there, painted a nondescript shade of gray or muted blue. It looked too purposeful, sitting with a settled stillness against the background clutter of the street.

And moving toward them, seemingly detached from the usual flow of pedestrian activity, was a man. He walked at an unhurried, measured pace, heading directly into their path of travel, appearing suddenly as if he had materialized from the side of the road, drawing both girls’ attention away from their conversation and towards the approaching presence of the van and the man.
